The IELTS Speaking test makes many people nervous. But it's the most predictable part of the exam: the format is always the same, and you know exactly what the examiner is listening for. Once you understand how it works, you can prepare in a smart way.

## How the test works

The Speaking test is a **face-to-face conversation with an examiner** and takes **11 to 14 minutes**. It is the same for IELTS Academic and IELTS General Training, and it is recorded. It has three parts.

| Part | Time | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Part 1**: Introduction and interview | 4–5 minutes | The examiner asks questions about familiar topics: your home, work or studies, hobbies, daily life. |
| **Part 2**: The long turn | 3–4 minutes | You get a task card with a topic. You have **1 minute to prepare** (you can make notes), then you speak for **up to 2 minutes**. |
| **Part 3**: Discussion | 4–5 minutes | The examiner asks more abstract questions linked to your Part 2 topic. You give opinions, compare and explain. |

## What the examiner is listening for

Your score is based on four things, and each one counts equally:

1. **Fluency and coherence**: Can you keep talking without long pauses, and are your ideas connected and easy to follow?
2. **Lexical resource**: Do you use a good range of vocabulary, including less common words and natural phrases?
3. **Grammatical range and accuracy**: Do you use different sentence structures, and how many mistakes do you make?
4. **Pronunciation**: Are you easy to understand? Do you use stress, rhythm and intonation naturally?

Notice what is *not* on the list: your opinions, your knowledge, or whether your story is true. The examiner is only assessing your **English**.

## 10 tips for a higher band

### 1. Extend every Part 1 answer

"Yes, I do" is too short. Aim for two or three sentences: answer, give a reason, add a detail or example.

> *Do you enjoy cooking?* "Yes, I really do, especially at the weekend when I have more time. Last Sunday I tried making a Thai curry for the first time, and it was surprisingly good!"

### 2. Use your Part 2 minute wisely

Don't write full sentences. Write **four or five keywords**, one for each point on the card, plus one or two good words you want to use. Then look up and speak.

### 3. Tell a story in Part 2

Stories are easier to talk about for two minutes than lists of facts. Use a simple structure: *when and where → what happened → how you felt → why it matters to you*. This is exactly the skill you practise when you retell a Bright Lives story.

### 4. Keep going until the examiner stops you

In Part 2, keep speaking for the full two minutes if you can. If you run out of ideas, add a detail, give an example, or talk about how you felt.

### 5. Give reasons and examples in Part 3

Part 3 is about ideas. Show that you can explain, compare and speculate: *"I think the main reason is…"*, *"Compared to twenty years ago…"*, *"In the future, it's likely that…"*

### 6. Buy time naturally

Everyone needs a moment to think. Use natural phrases instead of silence or "ummm":

- "That's an interesting question. Let me think…"
- "I've never really thought about that before, but…"
- "It's hard to say, but I suppose…"

### 7. Learn vocabulary by topic, in phrases

Common IELTS topics include work, education, technology, the environment, health, travel and family. For each one, learn natural **phrases**, not just single words: *"a healthy work–life balance"*, *"to cut down on sugar"*, *"to make a difference"*.

### 8. Mix your grammar

Try to use a range of structures naturally: past tenses for stories, conditionals (*"If I had more time, I would…"*), comparisons, and relative clauses (*"a friend who…"*). Don't force complex grammar. Accuracy matters too.

### 9. Don't memorise answers

Examiners are trained to notice memorised answers, and they sound unnatural. Instead, practise *talking about topics* so you can respond to any question.

### 10. Record yourself

Answer a practice question, record it on your phone and listen back. You'll hear your pauses, repeated words and pronunciation problems much more clearly than when you're speaking. Then try again.

## A 15-minute daily routine

- **5 minutes:** Answer three Part 1 questions out loud.
- **5 minutes:** Do one Part 2 card: 1 minute preparing, 2 minutes speaking, recorded.
- **5 minutes:** Listen back and write down three words or phrases that would have made your answer better.
